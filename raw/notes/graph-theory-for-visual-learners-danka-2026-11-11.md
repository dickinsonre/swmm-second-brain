## Proof / from scratch: what the post says, in order

1. **The motivating example.** The author's hometown is split by a river with two bridges; an accident on one bridge jams the whole city. Strip the map to intersections (nodes) and street segments (edges) and you have a graph. Traffic is a walk on it; close both bridges and the two halves are disconnected. Highlighting the streets that lie on many shortest paths shows the city's arteries. Graphs also model mathematical expressions, polyhedra and DNA assembly.
2. **What a graph is.** A set of nodes and edges. In a simple graph each edge is a two-element subset of the nodes; loops, weights and directions come later. A drawing is one of infinitely many pictures of the same graph; two drawings of one structure are isomorphic. The degree of a node is its number of incident edges. Degrees alone do not fix a graph: an eight-node cycle and two four-node cycles have every degree equal to two and are not isomorphic.
3. **Walks and paths.** A walk is an alternating sequence of nodes and edges and may repeat both; trimming the round trips gives a path, a walk with no repeated nodes or edges. Graph theory began with Euler's question of a walk across the seven bridges of Königsberg crossing each once, which is impossible; the post says graph theory is everywhere three hundred years later.
4. **Connectivity.** b is reachable from a if a path joins them. Reachability is an equivalence relation (reflexive, symmetric, transitive). A graph is connected when every node is reachable from every other; one walk that visits every node shows it. A graph that falls apart when one edge is removed is called 1-connected. The hometown graph falls apart when the two bridge edges are removed.
5. **Search.** A computer sees only nodes and their neighbor lists. Breadth-first search (BFS) explores level by level from a start node; depth-first search (DFS) goes as far as it can, then backtracks. Each finds one connected component; restart from an unvisited node to find the others. BFS from A gives a path to B with the fewest edges; DFS finds a path but possibly a long one.
6. **Cycles.** A closed walk ends where it started; a trail repeats no edge; a cycle is a closed trail with no repeated node. Removing any edge of a cycle leaves the graph connected, so cycles make a graph resilient. BFS and DFS find cycles by tracking visited nodes and their parents.
7. **Trees.** Cycles make paths between two nodes ambiguous and block a hierarchical ordering. A tree is a connected acyclic graph. Equivalent definitions: minimally connected (removing any edge disconnects it; the post's analogy is a power grid with no wasted lines); maximally acyclic (adding any edge makes a cycle); exactly one path between any two nodes. Any node can be the root, and levels are distances from it. DFS on a labyrinth drawn as a tree is the left-hand rule. Decision trees split the feature space into rectangles; the worked example asks x ≤ 0.13, y ≤ 0.09, x ≤ -0.94 and predicts yellow. The post says decision trees are state of the art on tabular data and win Kaggle competitions.
8. **Hamiltonian paths and cycles.** A Hamiltonian path touches every node exactly once; a Hamiltonian cycle also returns to the start. Neither need exist, and finding them is computationally hard. The edge graphs of all five Platonic solids have Hamiltonian cycles. In shotgun sequencing, DNA fragments become nodes, a directed edge joins two fragments when the end of one overlaps the start of the other, and a Hamiltonian path gives a candidate genome.
9. **Planar graphs.** A graph is planar if it can be drawn with no crossing edges; planarity belongs to the graph, not the drawing. K4 is planar; K5 is not; nor is K3,3, the utility graph (called the 3-houses-3-wells graph in Hungary). The edge graph of every convex polyhedron is planar. Euler's formula: v - e + f = 2 for a connected planar graph (nodes, edges, faces). Each edge borders two faces and each face has at least three edges, so 2e ≥ 3f, which with Euler's formula gives e ≤ 3v - 6. K5 has 5 nodes and 10 edges, and 3v - 6 = 9, so K5 is not planar. Subdividing an edge (adding nodes on it) keeps a nonplanar graph nonplanar. Kuratowski's theorem: a graph is nonplanar if and only if it contains a subdivision of K5 or K3,3; the post finds a subdivision of K3,3 in its DNA overlap graph.
10. **Vertex colorings.** Countries of Europe become nodes, neighbors become edges. A proper coloring gives neighbors different colors; the chromatic number χ is the fewest colors needed. Europe's map uses four, and Belgium, France, Germany and Luxembourg form a four-clique, so χ is exactly four. A complete graph needs as many colors as nodes, but a high chromatic number does not need a large complete subgraph: the Mycielskian construction, started from a five-node cycle, gives triangle-free graphs of any chromatic number. The four-color theorem: a planar graph has χ ≤ 4.
11. **Edge colorings.** A proper edge coloring gives the edges at each node different colors; the fewest colors is the chromatic index χ′. It is at least the maximum degree (Germany, with 9 neighbors, on the Europe graph). Vizing's theorem: χ′ is the maximum degree or the maximum degree plus one; even cycles take the first, odd cycles the second. A star needs as many edge colors as its center's degree but only two vertex colors.
12. **Ramsey theory.** Any two-coloring of the edges of K6 contains a one-color triangle; the post proves it with the pigeonhole principle instead of checking all 2^15 colorings. K5 can avoid one, so R(3) = 6. R(m) is the smallest n such that every two-coloring of Kn contains a one-color Km. R(1) = 1, R(2) = 2, R(3) = 6, R(4) = 18, the last known exactly; R(5) is between 43 and 46, R(6) between 102 and 160, R(7) between 205 and 492. Read as a social network: among 18 people, some 4 all know each other or all do not.
13. **Directed and weighted graphs.** A symmetric relation (friendship) gives unordered edges; a non-symmetric one (following, hyperlinks, one-way streets) gives ordered pairs, a directed graph. A function such as f(n) = n + 1 is a directed graph. A computational graph holds variables in nodes and operations as edges; logistic regression σ(ax + b) takes three steps; neural networks are computational graphs, and gradients can be stored on the edges. A weighted graph gives each edge a number; the hometown graph is weighted by street length, and shortest paths use the weights. A coin-flip walk is a random walk with probability 0.5 on each step. A Markov chain is a random walk on a directed graph whose weights are transition probabilities, described by a transition matrix; with arbitrary weights it becomes the adjacency matrix.
14. **Graph measures.** The long-run share of time a random walk spends at each node is the equilibrium distribution; the post says Google's PageRank computes this on the graph of websites. Betweenness centrality of a node v: for every pair s, t, the share of shortest s-t paths that pass through v, summed over all pairs. Edge centrality is defined the same way. Without traffic data, high-centrality nodes and edges point to likely bottlenecks; on the hometown graph the nodes and streets near the bridges score highest.
